#Report Although mental health has been a topic of great interest amongst researchers since the start of the 20th century, its importance and impact on people’s well-being has only recently started to be taken into account. People, hospitals, businesses, and governments have become more aware of people’s mental health, and so it is interesting to see how this has evolved over the past decades and how patients have been responding to this. NHS Scotland made available a dataset with information about mental health inpatients in hospitals in Scotland, dating back to 1997, which set the basis of this investigation. We decided to use variables such as age, sex and diagnosis to attempt to explain changes in the length of stay, as the latter may be the one impacted most by the advances of mental health awareness. This leads us to a guiding research question to be answered by this investigation: ‘to what extent has the length of stay of mental health inpatients in Scotland changed over the years 1997 to 2022?’

To explore this research question, our method was heavily reliant on data tidying and processing, since the data provided was quite messy. The main advantage with doing this is that we were able to make the application of data science much more straightforward and less convoluted. It would also allow us to more easily find correlations between length of stay and other useful variables, which may help answer the research question. Firstly, we had to load the data for 1997 and 2022, the more relevant years for our research, into separate objects using filter. Since the data was within the same dataset, this would make it much easier to work with and create useful results. Further, we had to translate the ICD10 codes for diagnoses into the common names and categories of the mental illnesses, since most would not know what each code stands for. A particular feature of this dataset is the number of continuous variables present, especially length of stay. Thus, we mutated each period of time into the midpoint value, e.g. 1-7 days was mutated to 4 days. This was done especially in order to allow us to find a linear model for the change in length of stay over time.

We opted to create several plots, visualisations and models as we wanted to see trends within categorical variables of length of stay, such as age, gender and diagnosis. Originally, we were meant to join the datasets of these different variables in order to investigate how they affect length of stay. However, we found out that there were no common rows between the datasets, meaning we could not join. Since we still wanted to explore these other variables, instead we chose to visualise age, sex and gender as sub-categories to see trends within these groups. The presentation shows eight visualisations, with most of them being bar plots, histograms or variations of that. We found these bar plots particularly useful for the variable we were investigating.
